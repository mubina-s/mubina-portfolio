07 / Reflections

What I’ve learned, and how I think about the work.

These reflections connect my coursework, engineering growth, communication, ethics, and the larger impact of technical decisions.

General Education Reflection

My general education coursework has broadened how I approach technical problems. English strengthened my research, technical communication, usability, and audience-awareness skills. Criminal Justice coursework strengthened my thinking about evidence, investigations, institutions, policy, and accountability. Together, these courses help me communicate technical work more clearly and consider the people and systems affected by security decisions.

Cumulative Reflection

My coursework has progressed from programming fundamentals into data structures, computer architecture, embedded systems, full-stack software, Linux, networking, and cybersecurity. That progression has changed the way I see security: vulnerabilities rarely exist in isolation. They emerge from interactions among code, hardware, networks, configurations, users, and organizational decisions. I want to continue developing the breadth to understand those layers and the depth to secure them responsibly.

Engineering Ethics

Ethics is part of how I think about security work: technical decisions affect privacy, access, evidence, safety, and trust. I want my engineering choices to be defensible not only because they work, but because they are responsible.

08 / Blog & Interests

Cybercrime notes, security questions, and interests beyond engineering.

A space for short writing about cybercrime and cybersecurity, plus the interests that shape how I look at technology and people.

Cybercrime note 01

Social engineering: the human side of security

Phishing and manipulation work because attackers target trust, urgency, and routine—not only software flaws. I’m interested in how technical controls, clear communication, and user awareness can work together to reduce that risk.

Security awareness • phishing • human factors

Cybercrime note 02

Digital evidence after an incident

Cyber incidents quickly become evidence problems. Logs, timestamps, system artifacts, and chain-of-custody decisions can affect what investigators are able to reconstruct and explain later.

Digital forensics • evidence • investigations

Cybercrime note 03

Ransomware is more than malware

Ransomware shows how security failures can cross technical and organizational layers. Backups, segmentation, identity controls, incident response, and communication all matter when systems are under pressure.

Ransomware • resilience • incident response
